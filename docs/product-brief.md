# Product brief

## Purpose

Help a person form a qualitative local weather outlook for the next 1 to 24 hours from their own observations. The questions include whether rain is becoming more likely or dry conditions may persist, whether wind may strengthen, ease or remain light, and whether conditions may warm, cool or stay similar. The useful forecast horizon depends on the evidence.

The encyclopedia supports independent exploration. Readers choose a phenomenon, follow connections and go as deep into atmospheric physics as they wish. A beginner should be able to start with an unfamiliar sky. Deeper explanations should reward an experienced amateur while keeping the focus on physical causes and observable consequences.

## Observation in the field

The usual starting point is a single look at the sky. A reader can compare what they see with atlas photographs and stop there. If interested, they can learn what change to look for next and return to the sky later. They make the comparison themselves.

The first release focuses on temperate plains and open water. Wind felt at the surface, relative warmth or cooling, and an optional personal barometer can add context. A pressure trend requires observations made at different times. The experience requires no external weather feed, radar, satellite imagery, specialized instrumentation, camera or computer vision.

## Learning outcome

On a later occasion, a beginner notices a familiar feature without the atlas and explains in their own words what it suggests about the weather or what change to watch for next. Recalling a cloud's formal name is secondary.

For example, a reader may recognize high, thin streaks and decide to watch for a spreading, thickening veil before drawing a conclusion about rain. A follow-up conversation after a new observation can demonstrate this outcome without an in-app quiz.

## Longer-term direction

The wider encyclopedia may cover atmospheric physics, wind, pressure, temperature, humidity, fronts, cyclones, anticyclones, precipitation, thunderstorms, fog, observation methods, forecasting and weather-data interpretation. Practical contexts include sailing, fishing and hiking.

Ideas for later consideration include interactive physical visualizations, recognition practice, situational exercises, observation journals and adaptive review. Their order and implementation remain open.
