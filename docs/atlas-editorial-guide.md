# Atlas editorial guide

## Collection

Prepare an entry for each of the ten cloud genera. The visual descriptions below are useful when sourcing photographs.

| Genus | Common description |
| --- | --- |
| Cirrus | High fibrous streaks or wisps |
| Cirrocumulus | A high patch or sheet of small ripples or grains |
| Cirrostratus | A thin high veil, sometimes with a halo |
| Altocumulus | Mid-level patches or rolls, often with shading |
| Altostratus | A broad grey or bluish layer, sometimes with a dimly visible sun |
| Nimbostratus | A thick layer associated with widespread steady precipitation |
| Stratocumulus | A low layer of rounded masses or rolls |
| Stratus | A low, fairly uniform layer |
| Cumulus | Detached heaps, often with flat bases |
| Cumulonimbus | A deep cloud tower, often with a fibrous or anvil-shaped top |

## Photographs

The [photo collection](photo-selection/README.md) contains the selected images and their credits. Source real photographs suitable for redistribution within an offline app. For every image, record the creator, original source, license or permission, required attribution and any modifications. Verify the identification against a reliable meteorological source.

Select a first image whose characteristic feature is easy to see on a phone. Prefer a frame filled by the cloud and sky alone, with a clear view of the identifying feature. Use the other two to show variation in lighting, distance, viewpoint or development while retaining a recognizable feature. Write a caption for each specific frame. When several genera appear together, identify where to look.

## Article coverage

- **Visible features.** Describe what the reader can check with their own eyes. Include a useful distinction from a similar-looking cloud where relevant.
- **Height and physical explanation.** Give typical heights for temperate latitudes, identifying whether a height describes the base, top or occupied layer. Connect the visible shape to a process in plain language.
- **Possible changes.** Describe plausible developments, including persistence or dissipation when relevant. Identify the next visible sign and link to another atlas entry when it helps the reader compare.
- **Weather implications.** Explain what the evidence supports about rain or continued dryness, wind and temperature. Use conditional language and an appropriate time horizon. Make clear when the available signs give little basis for an inference.
- **Other things to notice.** Explain relevant combinations with felt wind, relative temperature or personal barometer readings directly in the article. Mention differences between land and open water when they change the interpretation.
- **Related phenomena and optional history.** Connect an observable phenomenon, such as a halo, to its physical cause. Include an interesting fact, etymology or historical account when it adds understanding and has a credible source.

## Editorial example

A reader sees wispy cirrus. The wisps alone give little basis for a local rain forecast. If a high veil later spreads and the cloud cover thickens, the sequence can support an interpretation of an approaching frontal system. Point out what to look for in the cirrostratus entry and explain why the sequence matters. Avoid presenting a transition between cloud types as a guarantee of rain or a thunderstorm.

Choose combinations with a physical connection. A cool gust ahead of a nearby shower can come from descending air spreading along the surface. The outflow can reach the observer even if the rain passes to one side. That distinction helps a beginner use wind as additional evidence while understanding the limits of the observation.
